Shi'a Political Movements, 1936–2026

When the Ottoman Empire fell after the First World War, Britain and France drew new borders across the Middle East. Iraq and Palestine came under British rule. Lebanon and Syria came under French rule. Iran kept its own kings. Yemen kept its own imam in the north. In each of these places, Shi'a communities lived inside states that were not built around them, and often gave them little say. This page follows that history from 1936 to today, country by country: Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, and Yemen, and the neighbors around them. It also follows how these countries' politics came to support each other, and how their support for Palestine drew them into war with Israel and the United States. Three communities, three politics

Twelver Shiism is the school of Iran, Arab Iraq, Lebanese Shi'a, Bahrain, and Saudi Arabia's east. Zaydism is the school of northern Yemen and of the Houthis. The Alawites of Syria are a separate esoteric tradition; the Assad state was a military Ba'athist regime whose pact with Tehran was political. The four movements on this page are political movements with their own histories, and none of them speaks for every Shi'a community.

Where this gets overstated

Iranian links are real in Lebanon, Iraq, and Yemen, and they do not turn every Shi'a crowd into an Iranian one. Bahrain in 2011 was a domestic constitutional movement; the king's own commission did not find Iranian direction. The Green Movement and Woman, Life, Freedom were Iranian publics against their own government. Tishreen was an Iraqi public against Shi'a parties. Attributions of the 1983 Beirut bombings rest on American court findings and intelligence; Hezbollah denies them. Hama's toll is disputed. The 2026 war has no published ending, and rows that depend on one research institute's note are attributed in the event itself.
